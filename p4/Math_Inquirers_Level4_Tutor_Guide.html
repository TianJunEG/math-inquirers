Program guide for tutors: schedule, lesson routine, ten minute-by-minute lesson scripts with answer keys, a progress tracker and a parent summary. Math Inquirers is online first: every lesson runs live on video; the face-to-face notes are kept for partner centres.

The program at a glance

Level
Children whose school year covers Primary 4 topics (ages 10 to 11), going into P5, who enjoy thinking about maths. Every child gets the same pages and chooses how far to go
Length
10 lessons of 90 minutes, two a week over the 5 weeks of the holidays
Group size
Up to 8 children per tutor, live online. Pilot with 6. Add a second tutor or helper above 8.
Focus
Thinking skills: making a list in order, guess and check, drawing a model, working backwards, finding patterns, reasoning and explaining. Not speed drills.
The arc of every lesson
Start concrete with objects, record in a table, spot the pattern, state the rule, use it (often backwards), then explain why.
What children take home
A student workbook with an inquiry journal, 10 worksheets and a certificate
Before tutors start
Every tutor signs the tutor confidentiality agreement before receiving any materials.

Teaching habits that matter

  • Ask “How do you know?” after every answer, right or wrong.
  • Wait 10 seconds before helping. Let children struggle a little.
  • Praise the thinking (“You checked it two ways”), not just the answer.
  • Let children disagree with each other and settle it with objects or a drawing.
  • If a child finishes early, use the Challenge or Extension question, not extra drill.

Preparing the materials

  • Send the workbook PDF to parents one week before. Ask them to print it single-sided and the cut-out pages on card if they can.
  • Cut-outs used: digit, point, sign and bracket cards (Lessons 2, 3, 5, 10), square grid (Lessons 7, 8), dot grid and cube net (Lesson 8), graph grids (Lesson 9), fraction wall (Lesson 4), right-angle testers (Lesson 6), and a paper clock, pizza circles and what-to-bring page (Lessons 4, 6).   • Keep the answer key with the tutor only. Check answers together on screen.
  • Use the shared screen or a digital whiteboard for the mini-lesson table.

Setting up online

  • Send parents the workbook and materials list one week before. Needed: the printed workbook and cut-outs, scissors, a ruler, paper and a pencil.
  • Use one approved platform with breakout rooms and screen share. Admit only registered children and never share the link publicly.
  • Ask the parent to sit nearby but not give answers. The camera should show the child’s page and hands.
  • Hold up the answer page to the camera at the end of each lesson, or take a photo and send it through the approved channel.

How to use the scripts

Every lesson below is a minute-by-minute script. The slides match it: one slide or two for each part. You do not have to say the words exactly, but keep the questions, because the questions are what makes children think.

SAY
Words to say aloud, in plain language for 8- and 9-year-olds. Said in your own voice, not read from the page.
DO
What you do: set up, hand out, show a slide, or move around the room.
ASK / EXPECT
Questions to put to the group, with the answer you are listening for. Let children answer before you explain.
IF STUCK
A ladder of help. Give hint 1, wait ten seconds, then give hint 2. Do not give the answer.

Five habits that run through every script

  • Predict first. Before children test anything, ask for a guess and write it down. A wrong guess is useful because we can test it.
  • Wait ten seconds after every question. Count silently. Most children answer in the last four.
  • Ask “How do you know?” after right answers too, so that children do not read your face for the answer.
  • Praise the thinking, not the answer: “You checked it two ways” beats “Well done”.
  • When an answer is wrong, say “Let us test that,” and use objects, not “No”.

Answering a wrong answer

Lesson 1: Factor Hunters

Week 1 (first lesson) • Factors and multiples • Workbook: Lesson 1

Objective
Find all the factor pairs of a number by working in order, find common factors and common multiples to solve sharing and timetable puzzles, discover which numbers have an odd number of factors, and solve multiple-based logic riddles.
Thinking skills
Making a list in order, looking for a pattern, using clues one at a time, explaining why
Materials

DO Show the Sneak-peek puzzles slide. Give about 90 seconds per question. Question 1 uses objects, a drawing or counting, so every child can start. Question 2 is a thinking question. Do not teach and do not say who is right yet.

Answers are shown only to you: each child writes on their own paper and holds it to the camera at the same moment (or shows only you, on camera). Sneak-peek puzzle
Answer
Sneak-peek 1 (objects, drawing or counting): Ali has 12 sweets. He puts them into bags so that every bag has the same number of sweets, with none left over. Use counters or draw dots. Write ONE way he can do it, like 2 bags of 6.
Any one of these is right: 1 bag of 12, 2 bags of 6, 3 bags of 4, 4 bags of 3, 6 bags of 2 or 12 bags of 1. The numbers of sweets in a bag (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12) are the factors of 12. A child with one way has had a win: you may ask the whole group, can anyone find another?
Sneak-peek 2 (thinking): Two lifts. Lift A stops at every 3rd floor (3, 6, 9 ...). Lift B stops at every 5th floor (5, 10, 15 ...). Which floors from 1 to 40 do BOTH lifts stop at?
Floors 15 and 30. They are in both lists: they are the multiples of 15.

DO A good reason sounds like: A factor goes INTO a number (the sweets in each bag). A multiple is in a times table (the lift floors). Write both lists and look for what they share.

Answer
All the factor pairs of 36. Try 1, 2, 3 … in order: 1 × 36, 2 × 18, 3 × 12, 4 × 9. 5 is not a factor. 6 × 6. The pairs have met. How many factors? Draw a factor rainbow.
9 factors: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36.

SAY “The key idea today is: hunt in order. Factors: try 1, 2, 3 … in order until the pairs meet. Multiples: list them and look for the first number in both lists. Say it with me.”

DO Show the key idea slide at minutes 29 to 30. The “your turn” slides are optional: skip them if you are short of time. The “common mistakes” on that slide are for you, not the children. Watch for them during the worksheet:

  • Listing factors at random and missing one, or stopping too early (for 48, forgetting 6 × 8), instead of trying 1, 2, 3 … in order until the pairs meet.
  • Mixing up factors and multiples: saying 12 is a factor of 4, or listing 4, 8, 12 as the factors of 4.

Decide whether a shape made of 6 squares folds into a cube by folding it in your head, find which faces of a net are opposite (and use it for dice), complete a figure so that a given line is a line of symmetry, find all the lines of symmetry of a shape, and shade squares to make a pattern symmetric.
