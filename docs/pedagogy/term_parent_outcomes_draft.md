# Term program: what your child can do (parent-facing draft)

Draft for the owner, 23 Oct 2026. Plain English for Singapore parents. No prices, no promises about school results, no mention of any ministry, gifted programme or competition. Source for every line: `docs/pedagogy/term_review.md` section 5 (which traces each "I can" to specific lessons).

**Notes for the owner (delete before publishing)**
- Level 1 is written from the 40 built lessons. Levels 2 to 6 are written from the year plans; reread each block when its lessons are written, because titles and items may change.
- Every "I can" says what the lessons teach and practise. None says what a child will score. The standard closing line below should appear on every page that uses this text.
- Words kept off parent pages on purpose: exam, score, rank, "test" meaning an assessment, "MOE-aligned", "gifted", "SASMO", "GEP", "advanced", "heuristics". "Test it" and "test a claim" stay: they are the program's own slogan and a thinking move, not an assessment. "Bar model" appears once, explained, as the owner decided ("strips, called bar models at school").
- The first block of each level says in one plain line that some puzzles go past the school year on purpose, as optional challenge (owner decision of 22 Oct: say it plainly). Repeat the line on later blocks if you prefer.

**Standard closing line (for every level page):** *These are the things we teach and practise. Every child grows at their own pace, and a child who joins part-way sees the parts they attend. Mi's Challenge Quest at the end of each ten lessons is a game: nothing is marked, added up or compared, and your child's tutor sends you one line about what they saw.*

**How each lesson works (one line for parents):** every lesson has time to think before help, a chat where children give reasons ("I think ... because ..."), and a look-back where children say what was hard and what helped.

## Level 1 (ages 6-8)

### Lessons 1-10: Notice it
Children look closely at how numbers work. They build numbers from bundles of ten and single ones, find patterns on the 100 square, and learn several ways to add and take away within 100, then check their answers. Each lesson ends with a short chat where your child gives a reason. Some ideas go past Primary 1 on purpose, as optional challenge.

I can:
- show a two-digit number as tens and ones, find it on a number line, and say the number before and after it.
- list every number that fits a clue with digit cards, without missing one.
- find a pattern on the 100 square and say the rule.
- add and take away within 100 in more than one way, pick the quicker way, and check by adding back.
- say what the equals sign means, and why a total stays the same when I give some and take some.
- measure and draw a line in centimetres, and write one number sentence for each step of a story.

### Lessons 11-20: Test it
Children learn to test an idea, not just to give an answer. They find out who is where in a line, draw picture strips (called bar models at school) for "more" and "fewer" stories, and make equal groups and share fairly. They hear a claim, try it with an example, and learn that one example is not enough to be sure.

I can:
- say a place from 1st to 10th, counting from the front or the back.
- draw strips for a story and find how many more, how many in all, or how to make two amounts equal.
- double a number to add two close numbers quickly.
- test a claim with an example, find an example that breaks it, and say why.
- make equal groups, count in 2s, 3s, 4s, 5s and 10s, and share or group equally.
- find the number a shape stands for in a picture puzzle, and the price of one from the price of a group.

### Lessons 21-30: Explain why
Children use maths they will meet every day, and practise saying why. They tell the time, count money and find change, and list every way to do something so none is missed. They look for mistakes in sums and check an answer a second way.

I can:
- tell the time to 5 minutes, say am or pm, and find the time half an hour or an hour later.
- count money in dollars and cents, find change, and say how much more one price is than another.
- list every way to spend an exact amount, and check that no way is missing.
- solve a small grid puzzle by ruling things out.
- make a table to work out the 20th picture in a growing pattern.
- find the wrong step in a sum, say why, and check an answer a second way.

### Lessons 31-40: Choose and create
Children bring their tools together. They look at shapes, grids and graphs, count carefully, and then choose the way that fits a problem. The year ends with children writing a puzzle that has exactly one answer, and teaching one idea they liked.

I can:
- show halves and quarters of a shape, and find and count shapes inside a bigger shape.
- count the routes on a grid by listing them in order.
- make a picture graph, read one, and say what a graph cannot tell me.
- count every handshake or outfit without missing any.
- choose a way to solve a problem, solve it two ways, and check that they agree.
- write a puzzle with one answer and check a friend's puzzle.

### By the end of the year (Level 1)
Your child can start a short problem with a picture, objects, a list, a table or a pattern, choose between them, give a reason with an example, and check the answer a second way. They can add and take away within 100, tell the time, count money and read a picture graph.

## Level 2 (ages 7-9)

### Lessons 1-10: Notice it
Children take a fresh look at the numbers, shapes and measures of their school year. They learn quick ways to add in their head, share things fairly, work out shop bills in dollars and cents, and notice patterns. A few puzzles go past the school year, as optional challenge.

I can:
- put a three-digit number on a line and say it in words.
- add and take away in my head by splitting numbers and pairing friendly ones.
- share and group equally, and say what a left-over means.
- work out a shop bill in dollars and cents, and the change.
- name halves, thirds and quarters in any shape, and cut a shape into smaller shapes and count them.
- read a scale where only some marks have numbers.

### Lessons 11-20: Test it
Children learn how to check an idea. They read graphs and ask what a graph cannot tell them, test claims about odd and even numbers, draw strips for stories, read clocks to the minute, and list every way so none is missed. They use clues to rule things out.

I can:
- read a graph with a key and say what it cannot tell me.
- test a claim about odd and even numbers with many examples.
- draw a strip for a story, and explain a subtraction as the gap between two numbers.
- read a clock to the minute, and work out how long something took.
- list every way to do something so none is missed.
- use clues to rule things out, and write a puzzle with exactly one answer.

### Lessons 21-30: Explain why
Children practise giving reasons. They find the rule behind a pattern, place fractions on a line, work out what a shape is worth in a picture equation, and estimate before they check. They learn that one example that breaks a claim beats a hundred that fit it.

I can:
- find the rule behind a pattern whose steps keep growing.
- compare fractions on a line and add fractions with the same bottom number.
- find what each shape is worth in a picture puzzle.
- estimate with something I know (a metre, a kilogram, a litre), then check.
- solve long stories one step at a time with strips.
- find an example that breaks a claim, and say why a true claim is always true.

### Lessons 31-40: Create and connect
Children make up and mend problems. They try chance games and ask if they are fair, follow timetables and calendars, give directions on a grid, find mistakes in worked answers, and write problems for others. The year ends with a mixed set where they choose their own way in.

I can:
- tally a game and say whether it is fair.
- work out a fraction of an amount.
- read and write numbers to the thousands, and follow a timetable or a weekday cycle.
- find the wrong step in a worked answer and say why.
- write a problem that has exactly one answer.
- choose a way to start a mixed problem and say why.

### By the end of the year (Level 2)
Your child can choose a way to start an unfamiliar problem (draw it, list it, make a table or work backwards), say why in a sentence, and check it. They can work with three-digit numbers, money in dollars and cents, time to the minute, simple fractions and strips.

## Level 3 (ages 8-10)

### Lessons 1-10: Number, calculation and everyday tools
Children firm up number work they will use all year: big numbers on a line, written add and subtract with an estimate to check, multiplying by splitting a number into easy parts, and dividing with a remainder. They also learn to find the hidden question in a two-step story. Some quick-thinking tricks go past the school year, as optional challenge.

I can:
- place numbers up to 10 000 on a line.
- add and subtract with a written method, estimate first, and find the slip in a worked sum.
- multiply by splitting a number into easy parts.
- divide with a remainder and decide whether to round up.
- find the hidden question in a two-step story and draw a strip for it.
- compare and add fractions on a line, work with dollars and cents, and find the price of one.

### Lessons 11-20: Measurement, shape, data and patterns
Children learn to measure and compare sensibly: lengths, mass and volume, area and perimeter, graphs and patterns. They learn that area and perimeter are different things, and how a graph's scale can change what you notice.

I can:
- estimate with things I know and change between units.
- tell area from perimeter and show that one can change while the other stays.
- draw lines that are square to each other, or parallel, with a set square.
- read and draw a bar graph, and spot a misleading scale.
- find the rule of a pattern whose steps grow.
- plan a small shop within a budget.

### Lessons 21-30: Space, modelling and reasoning
Children look at shapes in space and learn to picture a problem. They fold and mirror shapes, draw strips for "times as many" and "before and after" stories, rule things out in a logic puzzle, and plan within a limit such as a budget or a fence.

I can:
- predict what a folded and cut paper looks like when opened.
- draw a strip for "times as many" and for "before and after" stories.
- work backwards from an answer to the start.
- find fractions of lengths, time and money.
- rule things out in a logic puzzle.
- plan within a budget or a fence and explain my plan.

### Lessons 31-40: Structure, counting and showcase
Children look for structure in numbers and learn to count carefully. They list factors, follow cycles, count outfits and handshakes without missing any, and learn to choose a way before they pick up a pencil. The year ends with children making up a problem and presenting it.

I can:
- list all the factors of a number.
- use a remainder to find where a repeating cycle lands.
- count outfits, posts and handshakes without missing or doubling any.
- guess, check and improve when there is a limit.
- choose a way to solve a problem and say what I decided not to do.
- write a problem and present it.

### By the end of the year (Level 3)
Your child can choose among drawing it, listing, making a table, finding a pattern and working backwards for a problem they have not seen, explain a step in writing, and check an answer by estimating.

## Level 4 (ages 9-11)

### Lessons 1-10: Number first
Children build a toolbox for working with big numbers, factors and fractions. They add and subtract across zeros, multiply in several ways, test whether a number divides evenly, and write fractions and decimals as each other. Some puzzles go past the school year, as optional challenge.

I can:
- add and subtract across zeros, and estimate first.
- multiply in more than one way and choose the neatest.
- test whether a number divides evenly by 2, 3, 5, 6, 9 or 10.
- build a fraction wall and find equal fractions.
- write a fraction as a decimal and a decimal as a fraction.
- divide with a remainder and say what the remainder means in the story.

### Lessons 11-20: Number in depth
Children go deeper on written methods, fractions and decimals. They multiply with a picture of rectangles, draw a strip for a story and change one part to see what changes, and add and subtract fractions and decimals. They learn to round a division sensibly.

I can:
- multiply two-digit by two-digit numbers with a picture of rectangles, estimating first.
- draw a strip for a story and change one part to see what changes.
- add and subtract fractions with different bottom numbers.
- find a fraction of what is left.
- use decimals in money and measures, and compare prices.
- divide and round the answer in a way that makes sense for the story.

### Lessons 21-30: Shape, space and measure
Children work with real shapes: nets that fold into solids, angles in degrees, area and perimeter, symmetry, and time on a timeline. They learn to find an unknown by subtracting a hole, or by cutting a shape and rebuilding it.

I can:
- match a solid to its net, and say which flat shapes fold into a cube.
- estimate, measure and draw angles in degrees.
- find all the rectangles with the same area, and which has the smallest perimeter.
- find area and perimeter of a shape with a hole or a border.
- find lines of symmetry and complete a figure.
- draw a timeline for how long something takes.

### Lessons 31-40: Data, moves and the road ahead
Children read data well, then learn named ways of solving hard problems one at a time: try a smaller problem, list in order, work backwards, and suppose then compare. The last lessons look ahead gently and end with a mixed set where they choose their own way.

I can:
- read a line graph, spot a misleading scale, and read a pie chart as parts of a whole.
- solve a smaller problem first to see the pattern.
- make a list in order so nothing is missed.
- work backwards to find where a story started.
- suppose, compare and adjust.
- choose a model or a way for a mixed problem and say why.

### By the end of the year (Level 4)
Your child can name and use the ways of solving problems they met this year (a smaller problem, a list, working backwards, suppose and compare, a strip), estimate before and check after, and give a reason in a sentence.

## Level 5 (ages 10-12)

### Lessons 1-10: Number and fractions
Children work with very large numbers, factors, fractions and decimals, and meet the order of operations. They learn to find the area of a triangle, even a tilted one, by drawing a box around it. Some ideas go past the school year, as optional challenge.

I can:
- read, compare and round numbers up to ten million.
- multiply and divide by 10, 100 and 1000, and say why digits move.
- list factors and multiples, and count the multiples of a number in a range.
- use the order of operations, and rearrange a calculation to make it easier.
- add, subtract and multiply fractions and mixed numbers, using a picture.
- find the area of a triangle, including a tilted one, and use angle facts about triangles.

### Lessons 11-20: Measure and make
Children measure and build in three dimensions. They look at solids from the top, front and side, find volume and the water level in tanks, work with decimals in measures, and learn about rates, such as two taps filling a tank together. They read graphs as short stories.

I can:
- picture a solid from the top, front and side.
- find volume, and the new water level after pouring.
- work with decimals in metres, kilograms and litres.
- find a rate, and add rates (a tap and a leak).
- read a graph as a story and spot a misleading scale.
- choose a way to start a mixed problem from a menu of ways.

### Lessons 21-30: Parts, percent and pattern
Children learn percent in real situations: discounts, tax and interest, and how to name the whole. They draw strips with units for unequal parts, undo steps from the end, and find the rule behind a pattern and why it must go on.

I can:
- find a percent of an amount, a discount, tax and a year's interest.
- name the whole when I say "this is a percent of that".
- draw a strip with units for unequal parts.
- undo steps from the end to find the start.
- find the rule behind a pattern and say why it must continue.
- count every possibility without missing or doubling any.

### Lessons 31-40: Shape, logic and strategy
Children explore angles in shapes and families of shapes, cycles in clocks and calendars, and logic puzzles. They learn to prove that something cannot happen, to break a long question into short ones, and to write a problem that has exactly one answer. The year ends with each child presenting a favourite problem.

I can:
- find the angles in a many-sided shape and sort four-sided shapes into families.
- rule things out in logic puzzles and number puzzles with hidden digits.
- show that something cannot happen, and try the extreme case.
- work backwards through fractions and percents, and break a long question into parts.
- write a problem with exactly one answer and show why.
- say which way I chose for a problem and why.

### By the end of the year (Level 5)
Your child can choose among about eight ways of solving a problem they have not seen, break a long problem into parts, give a one-sentence reason, and check an answer by estimating.

## Level 6 (ages 11-13, maths and simple programming)

*A note for parents of children in Primary 6: some lessons in Terms 3 and 4 fall in the busiest school months of the year. We will talk with you about which lessons to catch up on from the recording. Level 6 is built to run alongside school and to build ideas for the next stage; it is not a revision course.*

### Lessons 1-10: Build the number toolkit
Children look closely at numbers, fractions, decimals and percents, and at the structure inside numbers. They learn shortcuts and why they work, and begin to try out ideas with small computer programs. Some ideas go past the school year, as optional challenge.

I can:
- compare numbers with decimals and round them sensibly.
- order fractions, and change between fractions, decimals and percents.
- use shortcuts and divisibility tests, and say why they work.
- find the rule of a sequence where each term depends on the one before.
- take a fraction of an amount using a strip, and work backwards.
- change a small program and predict what it will do.

### Lessons 11-20: Scale and compare
Children learn how quantities change together: ratio, percent change, rates, circles, volume and scale. They use strips to share in a ratio and learn why going up 20% and then down 20% does not bring you back.

I can:
- share an amount in a ratio using strips.
- multiply and divide fractions with a picture.
- find a percent change, and the original price before a discount.
- combine rates, such as two workers or a tap and a leak.
- find the circumference and area of circles, and the volume of boxes.
- say how scaling changes length, area and volume.

### Lessons 21-30: Say it for every case
Children learn to write a rule with letters so that it covers every case. They solve equations, read straight-line graphs, work with powers and binary numbers, and check their rules with short programs.

I can:
- use rectangles to open out brackets and to pull out a common number.
- solve an equation, including when the unknown is on both sides.
- plot points and read a straight-line graph.
- work with powers and last digits.
- find which totals can be made, and why.
- build and explain a small puzzle-maker program.

### Lessons 31-40: What can we trust?
Children look at data, chance and logic, and ask what can be trusted. They learn that a chart can mislead, simulate chance games, count carefully, prove or disprove with an example, and finish with a project they plan, build, test and present.

I can:
- find an average, spot an outlier, and read a misleading chart.
- list the outcomes of a two-stage game, predict the result, then check it with a computer simulation.
- count arrangements and shapes systematically.
- find an example that shows a claim is false.
- choose among ways of solving a hard problem and say why.
- plan, build, check and present a project, and explain why it works.

### By the end of the year (Level 6)
Your child can pick a way to attack a problem they have not seen, justify the choice, try it out by hand and with a small program, and explain why the result is true.
