## Claims I could not confirm (not used, or used with a hedge)

- Ramanujan: the 1729 reply is Hardy's own recollection (Hardy 1940, p. 12 per Wikipedia; AMS blog gives no source), so labelled. The 'taught himself from Carr's Synopsis' detail has two descriptions (MacTutor says he found it at his school, a schoolfellow in Hardy's obituary says the College library), so the story leaves out where he found it. Childhood 'love of numbers' is not stated in the pages opened; I wrote 'loved maths'.
- Gauss: age at the school scene (7 vs 10 in sources) and the exact task are disputed. Hayes (American Scientist) finds the 1 to 100 series first named in 1938; the 1856 account names no numbers. So the story is labelled a legend and gives no age.
- Eratosthenes: birth and death years differ (276/194 BC vs about 285/205 BC). The sieve is only 'credited' to him; MacTutor says it appears in a later writer's book (the page names Nicomedes, not Nicomachus as I expected). The sieve procedure was described in one encyclopedia.com entry only, but it is easy to check by hand. Earth measurement is stated only as 'an estimate'; the stadium length is disputed.
- Ada Lovelace: her birthplace and her maths education (tutors) were not stated on pages I opened, so neither is claimed. 'First programmer' is debated by biographers (CHM says so), so not used. The loom sentence is paraphrased, not quoted, because sources differ on a hyphen (Jacquard loom / Jacquard-loom) and I could not open the original Note A.
- Al-Khwarizmi: birthplace uncertain (Khwarizm vs near Baghdad), religion uncertain, 'first' and 'introduced zero to the West' claims are unsourced or attributed to a secondary author, so left out. 'Father of algebra' left out (shared with Diophantus).
- Fibonacci: birth (1170 vs 1175) and death (1240s vs 1250) differ. His age in Bugia is unknown, so 'was taught there' only. The 'zero' detail was not confirmed. Money-exchange problems confirmed by MacTutor only; Knott's page does not mention them (it says he learned merchants' arithmetic).
- Ahmes: MacTutor dates him about 1680-1620 BC; the British Museum and Wikipedia date the papyrus copy to 1550 BC. The loaf-sharing problems (Problems 1-6) are in Wikipedia only, so the story says 'tables for dividing numbers' instead. The British Museum highlights page does not name the scribe; its collection page does (Ahmose).
- Archimedes: 287 BC birth year rests on a 12th-century writer (Tzetzes). Burning mirror, 'Eureka' crown story and 'place to stand' are traditions and left out. Death circumstances left out on purpose. 'Two thirds of the can' is the standard result for a ball inside a can of the same height and width (confirmed by Wikipedia-derived PDF and Eves notes; the ETSU notes give the formulas).
- Katherine Johnson: age at starting high school differs (10 in Britannica and CHM, 13 in NASA), so the story says 'early'. The Glenn quote differs in wording between pages, so it is paraphrased. The link to measurement is indirect (checking numbers), so I mark the lesson link as a moderate fit.
- Mirzakhani: Quanta quote about drawing came only via Artnet, so not quoted. Olympiad golds confirmed by Stanford Magazine and EMS; PBS says only 'two international awards'. Birthplace (Tehran) confirmed by PBS and Stanford News.

## Choices rejected and why

- Aryabhata and Brahmagupta: place-value and zero claims are contested for priority. Used Ramanujan for India instead.
- Hypatia and Emmy Noether: their stories are mainly about violence or persecution, and Noether's work is too abstract for a clear link at this level.
- Euclid: almost nothing reliable is known about his life, so no true story can be written. Used Archimedes for shapes (link is to solid shapes; the lesson itself is mostly 2D, so the link is a moderate fit).
- Pascal, Fermat, Descartes: little child-friendly effort story or link to these topics.
- Gauss for Challenge Day: kept for L2 where the link is direct.
- Weakest support: the 1729 taxi story rests on an AMS blog and Wikipedia (both pointing to Hardy's 1940 book, which I could not open), hence confidence medium and the legend label. Wikipedia is also a second source for the Rhind Papyrus alongside MacTutor and the British Museum.
