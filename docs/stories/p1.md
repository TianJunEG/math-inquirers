## Story text and sources

### Lesson 1: Katherine Johnson (NASA mathematician who loved to count)

Katherine Johnson grew up in West Virginia, in the USA. She said that she counted everything. She counted the steps to the road and the steps up to church. She counted the dishes she washed, too. She kept loving numbers and grew up to work at NASA. In 1961 her sums helped plan the path of a spaceflight for an astronaut called Alan Shepard.

Sources opened:
- https://www.nasa.gov/content/katherine-johnson-a-lifetime-of-stem
- https://www.biography.com/scientists/katherine-g-johnson

### Lesson 2: Carl Friedrich Gauss (German mathematician famous for clever number tricks)

A story often told about young Gauss goes like this. His teacher asked the class to add 1 + 2 + 3 and so on up to 100. Gauss paired the numbers: 1 and 100 make 101, 2 and 99 make 101, and so on. There are 50 pairs, so the answer is 50 times 101, which is 5050. Historians are not sure it happened exactly like this. But pairing numbers is a clever trick you can use too.

Legend note: Legend label used: 'A story often told about young Gauss ... Historians are not sure it happened exactly like this.' The 1-to-100 detail first appears in print long after his childhood.

### Lesson 3: Florence Nightingale (Nurse who used coloured charts to show numbers)

Florence Nightingale is best known as a nurse. She was also very good at statistics, which means collecting and sorting numbers. She turned her numbers into coloured pictures called charts, where a bigger wedge shows a bigger number. A chart helps people see the facts quickly. She used her numbers to show why hospitals needed to be cleaner.

Sources opened:

### Lesson 4: Fibonacci (Leonardo of Pisa) (Italian maths writer whose growing number pattern has his name)

Fibonacci lived in Pisa, in Italy, about 800 years ago. As a boy he travelled with his father, who was a merchant. He learned the number signs we use today, 0 to 9. In 1202 he wrote a big book to teach them in Europe. It has a puzzle about rabbits whose answers grow like this: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8. People named this pattern after him much later.

Sources opened:
- https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Strick/Fibonacci.pdf
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci

### Lesson 5: Al-Khwarizmi (Scholar in Baghdad whose book gave us the word algebra)

Al-Khwarizmi worked in Baghdad, in a place called the House of Wisdom, about 1,200 years ago. He wrote a book about finding an unknown number. One idea in it is balancing: when you do the same thing to both sides, the two sides stay equal. This helps you undo steps to find the hidden number. His book's title gave us the word algebra. We are not sure where he was born.

Sources opened:
- https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Al-Khwarizmi/
- https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Al-Khwarizmi

### Lesson 6: Isaac Newton (Famous scientist who also had a job looking after the country's coins)

Isaac Newton is famous for his big ideas in science. But for many years he also had a job at the Royal Mint, which is where English coins were made. He started there in 1696, when old silver coins were being worn out and replaced with new ones. The people at the Mint cared about every coin having the right weight and the right amount of metal. Newton worked there until 1727.

### Lesson 7: Maryam Mirzakhani (Mathematician who thought by drawing shapes and curves)

Maryam Mirzakhani was born in Tehran, in Iran. As a child she wanted to be a writer. Later she turned to maths, especially geometry, which is the maths of shapes. When she worked on a problem, she drew doodles on big sheets of paper and wrote ideas around the edges. Her daughter called it painting. In 2014 she became the first woman to win the Fields Medal, a top maths prize.

### Lesson 8: Leonhard Euler (Swiss mathematician who drew circles to show groups)

Leonhard Euler came from Switzerland. Starting in 1760 he wrote letters to a princess in Germany to explain science and ideas to her. In some letters he drew circles to show groups. A small circle inside a big circle shows a smaller group inside a bigger one. Many years later, people called these Euler's circles. Drawing groups as circles still helps us sort things and think clearly.

Legend note: Cats-and-animals is our own example, not from Euler's letters.

Sources opened:

### Lesson 9: Eratosthenes (Head of a great library who measured the whole Earth)

Eratosthenes was born in Cyrene, which is in Libya today. He worked at the great library in Alexandria, in Egypt, over 2,000 years ago. At noon in midsummer, the Sun was straight overhead in Aswan, far to the south. In Alexandria it was a little to one side. He compared the shadows and used the distance between the two cities. This let him work out how big the Earth is. His answer was quite close.

Sources opened:

### Lesson 10: Srinivasa Ramanujan (Indian mathematician who found surprising patterns in numbers)

Srinivasa Ramanujan was born in Erode, in India. He loved numbers and worked on maths from a young age. He wrote down his ideas and sent them in letters to mathematicians in England. One of them was G. H. Hardy. Later Ramanujan went to Cambridge to work with him. A story that Hardy told himself: he rode in taxi number 1729 and called it a dull number. Ramanujan said it was very interesting. Even a plain number can hide a surprise.

Legend note: Taxi story labelled as 'a story that Hardy told himself'; it is Hardy's own account of the conversation.

## Claims I could not confirm (so they were left out or softened)
- Gauss: the 1-to-100 story is a legend (American Scientist traces it to a 1856 memorial book that gives no numbers or pairing; the earliest source American Scientist found for the 1-to-100 version is from 1938). Age at the time varies by source. Story is labelled as a legend and the age is not stated. The pairing method itself is much older than Gauss, so no priority claim is made.
- Newton: his exact birth year differs by calendar (1642 or 1643), and no opened page stated it plainly, so only "1640s" is given. Mint sources agree on 1696 and 1727. His prosecution of coin forgers was left out as unsuitable for this age group.
- Euler: 1707-1783 appears on one opened page (Wikipedia, Euler diagram); the other opened page did not give dates. Please treat the years as medium-confidence until a second page is checked. His birthplace (Basel) was not on any opened page, so only "Switzerland" is used.
- Euler: the "princess" is named on one page only (Friederike Charlotte of Brandenburg-Schwedt); the story just says "a princess in Germany".
- Nightingale: "loved maths and asked her parents to study it" appears on MacTutor only, so it was left out. "First woman Fellow of the Royal Statistical Society (1858)" is on MacTutor only (nrich says she was nominated in 1858), so it was left out. Nurse role: MacTutor and nrich agree.
- Al-Khwarizmi: birth year, birthplace and even his dates are estimates in both sources, and historians disagree about how original he was. The story says "we are not sure where he was born". The link to "working backwards" is by analogy (undoing steps, doing the same to both sides). That is why confidence is medium. The word "algorithm" was left out as too hard.
- Fibonacci: the exact wording of the rabbit problem was not on the opened pages, so it is only described. Birth and death years are "about".
- Mirzakhani: Stanford Magazine and the NYT obituary (via MacTutor) agree on drawing on large sheets of paper. Her birth date differs between pages (12 May 1977 on Wikipedia, 3 May 1977 in the NYT piece), so only the year is used. "Loved reading" (NYT only) was left out.
- Katherine Johnson: NASA says she was in high school at about 10; Biography.com says she finished eighth grade by 10 and graduated college at 18. These do not agree on the details, so school facts were left out. Only "counted everything" (both pages) and the 1961 Alan Shepard flight (both pages) are used. The story says "she said" and gives no quotation.
- Ramanujan: the 1729 taxi conversation comes from Hardy's own account; it is labelled "a story that Hardy told himself". The "banana and zero divided by zero" school story (arXiv paper only) was left out. The date of the first letter to Hardy differs a little (late 1912 or 16 January 1913), so no date is given.
- Eratosthenes: how accurate his answer was depends on the length of a stadion, which is uncertain (sources give errors from under 3% to 17%). Story says only "quite close". Whether he measured the distance himself is not stated.

## Choices considered and rejected
- Emmy Noether: only wide, abstract links to P1 topics, and her later life includes persecution, so avoided.
- Ada Lovelace: a possible fit for Logic and Sorting, but her story and her father are not simple to tell at this age, and the popular claim "first programmer" is debated.
- Zu Chongzhi, Liu Hui, Aryabhata, Brahmagupta: little detailed biography to support two reliable sources for personal details, and no clear P1-topic link.
- Lewis Carroll (logic puzzles) and John Venn: would give a third English person; Euler was used instead for Logic and Sorting.
- Archimedes: linked to measuring, but the famous bath story is a legend and his death is not suitable for children.
- Gauss was kept for Number Bonds despite the legend, because the pairing idea is exactly a bond idea and the legend can be clearly labelled.
- Ramanujan's 1729 fact (smallest number that is a sum of two cubes in two ways) was left out of the story because cubes are too advanced for P1. It is available for a tutor aside.
