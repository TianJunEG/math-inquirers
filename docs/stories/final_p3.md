## Claims I could not confirm
- Whether Gerbert used a zero digit: sources say probably not; the story says historians think not.
- Montessori as "first woman doctor in Italy": sources disagree (Wikipedia notes an earlier graduate in 1877), so not claimed. Her fraction circles are described in Montessori teaching sources; I did not find a source dating their invention, so no date is given. She is a teacher and doctor, not a mathematician.
- Ibn al-Haytham: Britannica could not be opened (403); MacTutor does not state the equal-angle rule explicitly; Wikipedia credits him with a complete statement. The story says only that he studied reflection with angles, and notes earlier scholars knew the rule.
- Zu Chongzhi: death year (500 or 501) and calendar date (462, 465, adopted 510) vary across sources; the calendar is not dated in the story. Britannica not opened (403).
- Spear: the first-box-plot claim rests on Wikipedia, a University of Toronto exhibit and the RSS page; it is phrased as "some experts say". Her employers' full list not checked.
- Schickard: the year of the fire is not given in the sources, so none is stated; the Kepler-copy fire detail was left out.
- Uhlenbeck: the childhood reading line comes from Smithsonian only; Wikipedia page did not mention it (first 100k characters read). MacTutor's page for her was not found (the URL I tried was a different Uhlenbeck).

## Rejected choices
- Julia Robinson, Grace Hopper, Dorothy Vaughan, Mary Jackson, Ingrid Daubechies and others: already reserved at other levels.
- Pythagoras, Hypatia: avoided (legend-heavy or dark ending).
- Florence Nightingale and Fibonacci: used at Level 4.
- William Playfair (bar charts, Scotland): second Scottish choice plus a later-life story that is not child-friendly; Spear chosen instead.
- Lewis Carroll (logic puzzles): biographical controversies; not chosen.
