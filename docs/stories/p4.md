## Stories, try-its and sources

### Lesson 1: Factors and multiples - Sophie Germain (1776-1831)

Sophie Germain lived in Paris more than 200 years ago. As a teenager she began reading the books in her father's library, and she taught herself Latin and Greek so that she could read even more of them. Her parents were not happy about it, but she kept learning. Later she wrote to famous mathematicians using a man's name, M. Le Blanc. One of them, the great mathematician Gauss, praised her ideas about numbers, and later he found out who she really was. Sophie was fascinated by prime numbers, which have exactly two factors: 1 and themselves. She studied primes p where 2 times p plus 1 is also prime. Today these are called Sophie Germain primes. She also won a prize from the Paris Academy of Sciences for her work on how thin metal plates vibrate.

**Try it:** Take 7. Is 2 times 7 plus 1 a prime? List the factors of your answer to prove it. Then try 5.  
**Answer:** 2 x 7 + 1 = 15. The factors of 15 are 1, 3, 5 and 15, so 15 is not prime and 7 is not a Sophie Germain prime. For 5: 2 x 5 + 1 = 11, whose only factors are 1 and 11, so 11 is prime and 5 is a Sophie Germain prime.

**Icon idea:** A stack of old books with a small key tag showing 2p+1 on a number card (a pen-name mask is optional); not a portrait.

### Lesson 2: Numbers to 100 000 - Leonardo of Pisa (Fibonacci) (about 1170 - about 1240/1250)

Leonardo of Pisa, whom we now call Fibonacci, lived about 800 years ago. His father was a merchant official who worked in Bugia, a trading town in North Africa that is now Bejaia in Algeria. Leonardo went there as a boy and learned to count with ten digits, 0 to 9, where each digit's place gives its value. In 1202 he finished a book, the Liber Abaci, which showed merchants how to use these digits for money, trade and bookkeeping. Place value is what lets ten digits write any big number, such as 47 305. Leonardo did not invent these digits, but his book helped them spread across Europe.

**Try it:** Use the digits 7, 0, 4, 9 and 2 once each. Write the biggest 5-digit number and the smallest 5-digit number you can.  
**Answer:** Biggest: 97 420. Smallest: 20 479 (the 0 cannot go first, or it would not be a 5-digit number).

**Icon idea:** Ten digit tiles 0 to 9 sitting in labelled place-value columns, with a small merchant's scale beside them.

**Sources opened:**
- https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Fibonacci/
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci

### Lesson 3: Four operations - Katherine Johnson (1918-2020)

Katherine Johnson grew up in West Virginia in the USA. She was so quick at school that she was moved up several grades. She became a research mathematician at NASA's Langley centre in Virginia, where early on people who did calculations were called computers. She worked out the flight path for Alan Shepard's 1961 flight, the first American in space. In 1962 astronaut John Glenn was about to orbit the Earth. NASA now had electronic computers, but accounts say Glenn asked for Katherine to check the machine's numbers by hand. Her answers matched, and that helped everyone trust the plan. Checking an answer a second way is a habit every mathematician uses. She received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2015.

**Label to show:** Accounts say John Glenn asked for her to check the numbers. The exact timing is uncertain and the film Hidden Figures dramatised the scene, so do not describe it as in the film.

**Try it:** Check that 6 x 48 = 288 using a different method from the one you first used. (Hint: 48 is close to 50.)  
**Answer:** 6 x 50 = 300, and 6 x 2 = 12, so 6 x 48 = 300 - 12 = 288. It checks out. You can also check with division: 288 divided by 6 = 48.

**Icon idea:** A pencil resting on a sheet with a big green tick beside a column of sums, and a small looping orbit path drawn above it.

**Sources opened:**

### Lesson 4: Fractions - Ahmes (about 1550 BC (the date of his copy of the papyrus))

About 3,500 years ago in Egypt, a scribe named Ahmes wrote out a long maths book on a roll of papyrus. We call it the Rhind Papyrus, named after the man who bought it in Luxor in 1858. Ahmes copied it from an older work. The book has problems about food, fields and building. It also has a big table that writes fractions with 2 on top, such as 2 out of 3, 2 out of 5 and 2 out of 7, by using unit fractions, which have 1 on top. The table goes all the way up to 2 out of 101. Parts of the papyrus are now kept in the British Museum in London.

**Try it:** Egyptian scribes liked unit fractions. Write 5/8 as two different unit fractions added together. (Hint: start with 1/2.)  
**Answer:** 5/8 = 1/2 + 1/8, because 4/8 + 1/8 = 5/8.

**Icon idea:** A rolled papyrus scroll with the fractions 1/2 and 1/4 drawn on it in a simple reed-pen style, plus a small bread-loaf cut into pieces.

**Sources opened:**
- https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Ahmes/
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhind_Mathematical_Papyrus

### Lesson 5: Decimals - Simon Stevin (1548-1620)

Simon Stevin was born in Bruges, in what is now Belgium, in 1548. He worked on many things, from trigonometry to the science of water and weights. In 1585 he published a small book called The Tenth (La Thiende) about decimal fractions. Stevin did not invent decimals. Others had used them before him. What he did was explain them clearly for the people who needed them. He said they would be useful for surveyors, carpet-makers, wine-gaugers, mint-masters, stargazers and every kind of merchant. He showed how to split a whole into tenths, hundredths and thousandths and how to calculate with them. We still use decimals this way for money and measuring today.

**Try it:** Write 3 + 4/10 + 5/100 as a decimal. Then add 2.8 to your answer.  
**Answer:** 3 + 4/10 + 5/100 = 3.45. Then 3.45 + 2.8 = 6.25.

**Icon idea:** A ruler marked in tenths with a bold decimal point, next to a small tape measure and a coin; not a portrait.

**Sources opened:**
- https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Stevin/
- https://www.britannica.com/print/article/565994

### Lesson 6: Angles - Eratosthenes (about 276 BC - about 194 BC)

Eratosthenes was a Greek scholar who worked in the great library of Alexandria in Egypt, over 2,000 years ago. He heard that at noon on one day of the year the Sun shone straight down a deep well in the town of Syene, so nothing had a shadow. On that same day in Alexandria, a tall upright stick did make a shadow. Eratosthenes worked out that the shadow's angle was about 7.2 degrees, which is one fiftieth of a whole turn. That told him the two towns were one fiftieth of the way round the Earth. People had measured the distance between them as about 5,000 stadia, so he multiplied by 50. His answer, about 250,000 stadia, was surprisingly close to the real size. Historians cannot be sure how long his stadion was, so we cannot say exactly how close.

**Label to show:** The exact length of a stadion is uncertain, so how close his answer was is uncertain too.

**Try it:** A whole turn is 360 degrees. If a shadow made an angle of 10 degrees, how many angles that size would fit all the way round?  
**Answer:** 360 divided by 10 = 36, so 36 angles. (Eratosthenes had about 7.2 degrees, and 360 divided by 7.2 = 50.)

**Icon idea:** An upright stick on flat ground casting a short shadow, with a sun and a small angle arc drawn between the stick and a sunbeam.

**Sources opened:**
- https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/global/learning-lesson-shadow-knows-i
- https://www.historyskills.com/classroom/ancient-history/eratosthenes-earth

### Lesson 7: Area and perimeter (rectangles and squares) - Liu Hui (lived in the 3rd century AD (exact dates unknown))

Very little is known about Liu Hui's life, except that he lived in China in the 3rd century AD. What we do know is his work. China had an old maths book called The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art. Its first chapter gives rules for finding the area of fields shaped like rectangles, triangles, trapeziums and circles. Liu Hui wrote notes on the book. He did not just say, use this rule. He worked to show why each rule gives the right answer, and he did it with careful reasoning about shapes. That asking why is a big part of being a mathematician. When you explain why length times width gives the area of a rectangle, you are doing what Liu Hui did.

**Try it:** A rectangular field is 12 m long and 5 m wide. Find its area and its perimeter. Then explain why the area rule works using rows of squares.  
**Answer:** Area = 12 x 5 = 60 square metres. Perimeter = 12 + 5 + 12 + 5 = 34 m. The field is 5 rows of 12 squares, so the squares can be counted as 5 groups of 12, which is 60.

**Icon idea:** A brush pen beside a bamboo scroll showing a small field drawn as a rectangle on a square grid.

**Sources opened:**

- https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Liu_Hui.pdf
- https://old.maa.org/press/periodicals/convergence/on-squares-rectangles-and-square-roots-square-roots-in-ancient-chinese-mathematics

### Lesson 8: Symmetry and nets - Emmy Noether (1882-1935)

Emmy Noether was born in Erlangen, Germany, in 1882. When she wanted to study mathematics at university, women were only allowed to sit in on classes if the teacher gave permission. She kept going, and in 1907 she earned her doctorate. For years she worked without pay. Later, she was invited to Göttingen, where for a time her lectures had to be advertised under another professor's name. Still she carried on. In 1918 she proved a famous idea now called Noether's theorem. It says that when something in nature has a kind of symmetry, there is a matching quantity that stays the same. One example is that energy stays the same when the rules do not change over time. Symmetry is not only pretty. It tells us deep things.

**Try it:** How many lines of symmetry does a square have? How many does a rectangle that is not a square have?  
**Answer:** A square has 4 lines of symmetry (2 through the middles of opposite sides and 2 along the diagonals). A rectangle that is not a square has 2 (through the middles of opposite sides).

**Icon idea:** A butterfly with matching wings and a dotted mirror line down the middle, sitting on a small chalkboard.

**Sources opened:**
- https://britannica.com/print/article/417132
- https://www.peba.kit.edu/english/6776.php

### Lesson 9: Tables and line graphs - Florence Nightingale (1820-1910)

Florence Nightingale was born in Florence, Italy, in 1820. She is best known as a nurse, but she also loved numbers and statistics. During the Crimean War in the 1850s she collected figures about the hospitals for soldiers. They showed that many more soldiers became sick from dirty, crowded conditions than from the fighting. She knew that long lists of numbers are hard to read, so she drew special charts to make the facts easy to see. Her ideas helped to bring in cleaner, safer hospitals. In 1858 she became the first woman to be elected a Fellow of the Statistical Society of London, now called the Royal Statistical Society. She showed that a clear table or graph can help people make better decisions.

**Try it:** A table shows the temperature: 9 am 26 degrees, 12 noon 31 degrees, 3 pm 33 degrees, 6 pm 29 degrees. Between which two times did it rise the most, and by how much?  
**Answer:** 9 am to 12 noon: it rose by 5 degrees (31 - 26). Noon to 3 pm: it rose by only 2 degrees. So the biggest rise was 9 am to 12 noon, by 5 degrees. (Between 3 pm and 6 pm it fell by 4 degrees.)

**Icon idea:** A small oil lamp beside a simple line graph with a rising and falling line, drawn in friendly flat colours; not a portrait.

**Sources opened:**
- https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Nightingale/
- https://nrich.maths.org/4790

### Lesson 10: Mixed heuristics (challenge day) - Maryam Mirzakhani (1977-2017)

Maryam Mirzakhani grew up in Tehran, Iran. She loved mathematics, and she was the first girl to join Iran's team at the International Mathematical Olympiad, a competition for school students from around the world. She won a gold medal in 1994. She went back in 1995, got a perfect score, and won gold again. Later she studied at Harvard University in the USA and became a professor at Stanford University. She has been described as a slow thinker who took her time over each problem. Her work on hard problems about shapes won her the Fields Medal in 2014. She was the first woman to win it. Her story shows that patience and trying different ways in can solve big problems.

**Try it:** A farmer has hens (2 legs) and rabbits (4 legs). There are 5 animals and 14 legs in all. How many of each? Try guess and check, or suppose they are all hens.  
**Answer:** 3 hens and 2 rabbits. Check: 3 x 2 + 2 x 4 = 6 + 8 = 14 legs and 3 + 2 = 5 animals. (If all 5 were hens there would be 10 legs. The extra 4 legs mean 2 animals are rabbits, since each rabbit adds 2 extra legs.)

**Icon idea:** A gold-coloured medal beside a notebook page with a path of loops and arrows drawn inside a polygon; not a portrait.

**Sources opened:**
- https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Mirzakhani/
- https://stanfordmag.org/contents/virtuoso-professor-saw-the-beauty-of-math

## Claims I could not confirm (left out or softened)

- Sophie Germain: the Academy prize year differs between sources (MacTutor places it in the 1815 contest, Wikipedia says 8 January 1816), so no year is given. "First woman to win an Academy prize" is only in Wikipedia, so it is left out. The Sophie Germain prime definition (2p+1 prime) is stated by Wikipedia; MacTutor describes her "auxiliary primes" 2Np+1, and Britannica does not give the definition. The family anecdotes (candle, cold room, frozen ink) and the Archimedes inspiration story are unsourced anecdotes and are left out.
- Fibonacci: birth and death dates are approximate (MacTutor 1170-1250 but notes c.1240; Wikipedia 1240-1250). The name "Fibonacci" was not used by him. The portrait is not authentic. The original 1202 manuscript is not known to survive.
- Katherine Johnson: the year she joined Langley differs (1952 on NASA, 1953 in Science News), so it is omitted. "Finished high school at 14" is on the NASA page only, so it is replaced by "moved up several grades" (NASA and Casio). The exact date and wording of Glenn's request are uncertain and the film dramatised it. The Casio page and the Science News page repeat an unsourced "if she says they're good" line, so it is not used as a quotation.
- Ahmes: MacTutor dates his life to about 1680-1620 BC, Wikipedia dates the papyrus copy to about 1550 BC, so the story says only "about 3,500 years ago". MacTutor says the British Museum got it in 1863, Wikipedia says 1865, so no date is given. The "only a scribe" remark is on MacTutor only and is left out.
- Simon Stevin: MacTutor says Arabic and Chinese mathematicians used decimals earlier; this is not in the second source, so the story says only "others had used them before him". The claim that his notation was taken up by Clavius and Napier, and the Jefferson link, are not used. The title appears as "La Thiende" (MacTutor, Britannica) and "De Thiende" (Wikipedia).
- Eratosthenes: the length of the stadion is uncertain, so no kilometre figure is given. Details of the well at Syene come from sources that do not cite the ancient writers directly.
- Liu Hui: nothing is known of his life (DSB). Britannica dates his commentary to 263, but the DSB says 263 belongs to another work (the Sea Island Mathematical Manual), so only "3rd century AD" is used. No source I opened describes a cut-and-rearrange area proof for rectangles, so the story says only that he explained why the area rules work.
- Emmy Noether: Britannica has a small date typo for a title; not used. Her 1933 dismissal and death are not included, because the brief asks for no heavy detail.
- Maryam Mirzakhani: her doodling on large sheets of paper is only in Stanford Magazine, so it is left out. "Slow thinker" is in MacTutor (as her own description) and Stanford Magazine ("slow"), but no verbatim quote is used. MacTutor says some references give 3 May for her birthday and that this is wrong; no birth date is stated. Her death in 2017 is shown in the years only.
- Florence Nightingale: the figures in the MacTutor page for January 1855 do not agree with each other, so no numbers are quoted. "Seven times more soldiers died of disease than battle" is unsourced and left out. The 12-wedge description of her diagram is only on the NRICH page, so it is left out. The claim that she was Sylvester's best pupil is hearsay and left out.
- General: this environment let me open only pages that appeared in search results, so Wikipedia is used as the second source in a few places (Germain, Fibonacci, Ahmes). If you want only museum, university or Britannica sources, replace these before printing.
