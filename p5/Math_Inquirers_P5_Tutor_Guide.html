Program guide for tutors: schedule, lesson routine, ten minute-by-minute lesson scripts with answer keys, a progress tracker and a parent summary. Math Inquirers is online first: every lesson runs live on video; the face-to-face notes are kept for partner centres.

The program at a glance

Level
Children finishing Primary 5 (ages 11 to 12), going into P6, the PSLE year, who are ready for more thinking than standard practice
Length
10 lessons of 90 minutes, two a week over the 5 weeks of the holidays
Group size
Up to 8 children per tutor, live online. Pilot with 6. Add a second tutor or helper above 8.
Focus
Thinking skills: making a list in order, guess and check, drawing a model, working backwards, finding patterns, reasoning and explaining. Not speed drills.
The arc of every lesson
Start concrete with objects, record in a table, spot the pattern, state the rule, use it (often backwards), then explain why.
What children take home
A student workbook with an inquiry journal, 10 worksheets and a certificate

Teaching habits that matter

  • Ask “How do you know?” after every answer, right or wrong.
  • Wait 10 seconds before helping. Let children struggle a little.
  • Talk time is by invitation: ask for volunteers first, never cold-call a child by name, always allow “pass”, and never keep public tallies or rankings.
  • Praise the thinking (“You checked it two ways”), not just the answer.
  • Let children disagree with each other and settle it with objects or a drawing.
  • If a child finishes early, offer the Go further what-if or the Investigation, not extra drill.

Preparing the materials

  • Send the workbook PDF to parents one week before. Ask them to print it single-sided and the cut-out pages on card if they can.
  • Cut-outs used: square grid and dot grid (Lessons 4, 8), fraction wall (Lessons 2, 3), cube net (Lesson 5). Children need a protractor and a ruler.
  • Keep the answer key with the tutor only. Check answers together on screen.
  • Use the shared screen or a digital whiteboard for the mini-lesson table.

Setting up online

  • Send parents the workbook and materials list one week before. Needed: the printed workbook and cut-outs, scissors, a ruler, paper and a pencil.
  • Use one approved platform with breakout rooms and screen share. Admit only registered children and never share the link publicly.
  • Ask the parent to sit nearby but not give answers. The camera should show the child’s page and hands.
  • Hold up the answer page to the camera at the end of each lesson, or take a photo and send it through the approved channel.
  • Lessons are recorded on the approved platform so that children who miss one can catch up; follow “Recording the lesson” in this guide. Do not make your own recordings or photos. Add a second tutor or helper above 8 children.

How to use the scripts

Every lesson below is a minute-by-minute script. The slides match it: one slide or two for each part. You do not have to say the words exactly, but keep the questions, because the questions are what makes children think.

SAY
Words to say aloud, in plain language for 10- to 12-year-olds. Said in your own voice, not read from the page.
DO
What you do: set up, hand out, show a slide, or move around the room.
ASK / EXPECT
Questions to put to the group, with the answer you are listening for. Let children answer before you explain.
IF STUCK
A ladder of help. Give hint 1, wait ten seconds, then give hint 2. Do not give the answer.

Five habits that run through every script

  • Predict first. Before children test anything, ask for a guess and write it down. A wrong guess is useful because we can test it.
  • Wait ten seconds after every question. Count silently. Most children answer in the last four.
  • Ask “How do you know?” after right answers too, so that children do not read your face for the answer.
  • Praise the thinking, not the answer: “You checked it two ways” beats “Well done”.
  • When an answer is wrong, say “Let us test that,” and use objects, not “No”.

Sneak-peek puzzles, choosing a start and the reflection

  • Every lesson starts with 3 minutes of sneak-peek puzzles: two puzzles on ideas we have not learnt yet. The first is an access puzzle any child can try with objects, drawing or counting, so everybody starts with a win. The second is a thinking puzzle. Say clearly: guessing and “I do not know” are welcome and nobody is marked.
  • Answers are shown privately: on the child’s own paper held to you as you walk round. Online, everyone holds their paper up to the camera at the same moment, or shows it to you only, on camera. Never use boards in front of the group, never read answers out with names, never keep a tally or a ranking. Ask for volunteers to say how they thought.
  • Choosing a start: every child has the same neutral line on workbook page 1, “After question 3 you may choose: carry on, or try Think harder.” Invite the WHOLE group, never one child: "when you have finished Start here you can choose Think harder, Go further or the Investigation." Never announce who is on which page, never name a child in front of the group for this, and do not split a pair unless both children agree. Any child may swap at any time. The Go further what-if on the explore slide and the Investigation (printed on the page every child gets) give anyone more to do without waiting.
  • Every lesson ends with a 2-minute reflection in a small box on the Investigation and reflection page: “Something I did well”, “The hardest part was...”, and “What helped me” ticks that include “I asked for help” and “I am still stuck (my tutor will check in with me)”. Pass is always allowed. It is never collected or read out in public; you may read it privately to plan the next lesson, and you check in with anyone who ticked “still stuck” in the open session (a quick visit to the shared breakout room or a hand signal, with a parent nearby, never a private message). Praise strategy, not speed.

Recording the lesson

Every live lesson is recorded so that a child who misses it can still catch up. A child watching alone cannot ask you a question, so the recording needs spoken cues, visible timers and quiet working time.

How to teach so the recording works

  • Say the short REC line at the start of each part, once, to the camera, in a friendly “recording voice”. Keep it under ten seconds. The children in the live room will simply hear it as a routine.
  • Say the workbook page and question number aloud every time, and read each question out loud as well as showing it.
  • Put a countdown timer or a clock slide on screen while children work, and say “the timer is running” so the recording shows how long to pause.
  • Repeat a child’s answer in your own words before you respond, so it is clear on the recording without relying on their voice.
  • Do not talk through the working time. Silence is fine; the child watching later pauses the video anyway.
  • Show the answer slides clearly and read each answer aloud, so a child can mark their own work.

Privacy and consent

  • Only record on the approved platform, and only with parent consent given at registration. Do not make your own recordings or photos.
  • Record the tutor and the shared screen only. Turn off or exclude the other children’s video in the recording (use active-speaker or screen-only recording), and keep children’s names off the screen.
  • Do not read out a child’s name together with a right or wrong answer. Do not show a child’s page to the camera in the recording.
  • Share the recording only through a private link to enrolled families, and delete it when the program ends, or after the period the parents agreed to.
  • Tell the group at the start of each lesson that it is being recorded and what is recorded. A parent or child who does not wish to be in it can keep the camera off.

These are suggested working rules. Make sure the parent consent wording, the tutor agreement and the platform settings match them before the first recorded lesson.

After the lesson

  • Add a chapter marker at each time in the chapter list at the end of the lesson, so a child can jump to the part they need.
  • Turn on captions if the platform offers them.
  • Put the workbook page numbers and the materials list in the description of the recording.

Investigation
Detail
Investigation (printed on the Investigation and reflection page every child gets): Million Puzzles (What if we change the target?)
First step for everyone: Easy first step: use the cards 0, 1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 8 for just ONE target, 4 000 000. Write one number from just below it and one from just above it. Which is closer? What to look for / model answer
Closest: 3 000 000 gives 2 875 410 (below, 124 590 away); 4 000 000 gives 4 012 578 (above, 12 578); 5 000 000 gives 5 012 478 (above, 12 478); 6 000 000 gives 5 874 210 (below, 125 790); 7 000 000 gives 7 012 458 (above); 8 000 000 gives 8 012 457 (above). Look for: both sides tested every time, gaps compared by subtracting, a rule in words.

Investigation
Detail
Investigation (printed on the Investigation and reflection page every child gets): Challenge Day (Find the pattern)
First step for everyone: Easy first step: with 1 step there is 1 way. With 2 steps there are 2 ways (1 + 1, or 2). Draw the ways for 3 steps first. (a) Ali climbs a staircase and takes either 1 step or 2 steps at a time. How many different ways can he climb a staircase with 1 step? 2 steps? 3 steps? 4 steps? List every way for 4 steps (for example 1+1+2 and 2+1+1 are different). (b) Make a table up to 6 steps. What pattern do you see? Explain why it works (hint: think about the LAST jump Ali makes). (c) Use your pattern to predict the number of ways for 8 steps. What if Ali could also take 3 steps? Try 3 and 4 steps again.
What to look for / model answer
Part 1: 1 step: 1 way; 2 steps: 2 ways (1+1, 2); 3 steps: 3 ways (1+1+1, 1+2, 2+1); 4 steps: 5 ways (1+1+1+1, 1+1+2, 1+2+1, 2+1+1, 2+2). Part 2: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13: each is the sum of the two before it. Reason: the last jump is 1 step (so Ali had climbed n − 1 steps before) or 2 steps (n − 2 steps before), so the ways for n steps = ways for n − 1 + ways for n − 2. Part 3: 8 steps gives 34 ways (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34; checked by brute force). With 3-steps allowed: 3 steps has 4 ways and 4 steps has 7 (each is the sum of the three before). Look for: a complete, ordered list, the table, and the last-jump reasoning.
