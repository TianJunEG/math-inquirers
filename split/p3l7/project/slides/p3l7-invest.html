Investigation: What if we change it? What stays the same?

A square with sides 4 cm has area 16 cm² and perimeter 16 cm. Make it 1 cm longer one way and 1 cm shorter the other way. You get a 5 cm by 3 cm rectangle.

1. Work out the area and perimeter of the 5 by 3 rectangle. What changed? What stayed the same?

2. Do the same for a 6 cm square (7 by 5) and a 10 cm square (11 by 9). Fill in the table. Then choose a square of your own.

3. Is it always true? Draw the 4 by 4 square and explain what happens to the area.

Easy first step: work out the area and perimeter of the 4 by 4 square and of the 5 by 3 rectangle. A table for your answers is on the Investigation page of the workbook.

Do not say who is on which one. Not finished at 72? Take it home and bring one thing to share at the start of next lesson (volunteers only). DEBRIEF in talk time, minutes 72 to 75: ask for volunteers to share one thing they noticed (a pass is fine). Then show the model answer and ask "What stayed the same? What changed? Why?" Model answer and what to look for: 4 by 4: perimeter 16, area 16 becomes 5 by 3: perimeter 16, area 15. 6 cm: 24 and 36 become 7 by 5: 24 and 35. 10 cm: 40 and 100 become 11 by 9: 40 and 99. The perimeter stays the same and the area is always exactly 1 cm² smaller. This is true for every square (checked by computer for sides 2 to 499). Why: move the bottom row of 4 squares to the right side. The new column is only 3 squares tall, so 3 squares fit and 1 is left over. Look for: several tests, the pattern 'area drops by 1', and any picture or cut-and-move reason. Extension: what if we make it 2 cm longer and 2 cm shorter? (area drops by 4; perimeter still the same).
