Investigation: Odd or even?

A puzzle with more than one good answer. Share one way you tried it, or pass.

Ali says: "When I add two odd numbers, I always get an even number."

(a) Test Ali's idea with 5 pairs of odd 3-digit numbers (for example 345 + 271).

(b) Now test odd + even, and even + even. What do you notice?

(c) Explain WHY, using pairs of counters or a drawing.

Easy first step: use counters to try 3 + 5, 7 + 9 and 11 + 13. Are the answers odd or even?

Math Inquirers Level 2 Holiday Program • Notice it. Test it. Explain why.

Minutes 77 to 80 (3 minutes, after talk time). Investigation debrief. The Investigation is printed on the last core page of the workbook; children may have started it any time after question 3 (it is open to everyone), or it is take-home. Say to the whole group: "Who would like to share one way they tried the Investigation? Passing is fine." Ask for volunteers only; never name a child; no public tallies, scores or rankings. Several answers are right: praise the method (a list in order, a table, a drawing), not the speed. If nobody has started it, show the first step on the slide and let pairs try for one minute, then set it as take-home. Model answer and what to look for: (a) Ali is right: odd + odd is always even (checked for every pair of 3-digit numbers). (b) Odd + even is always odd; even + even is always even. (c) Why: an odd number is some pairs plus 1 left over, an even number is only pairs. Two odd numbers have two left-overs, and they make one more pair, so no one is left over. Look for: several tests before a claim, then a reason that uses pairs, counters or ones digits, not only &quot;it worked every time&quot;. A ones-digit reason (odd ends in 1, 3, 5, 7, 9) is also fine. Extension (not printed): taking away behaves the same way: odd − odd is even, odd − even is odd.
