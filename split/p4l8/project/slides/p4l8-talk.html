Mei says: "Every shape made of 6 squares folds into a cube." Ben says: "If a shape has 4 squares in a row, it always folds into a cube." Who is right?

Sentence starters:

“I noticed that …” “I tested it by …” “I know because …”

Pass is always fine. Volunteers share first. We do not keep scores.

Math Inquirers P4 Holiday Program • Notice it. Test it. Explain why.

Minutes 80 to 84. Pair talk is 1 minute (no breakout rooms: children tell the grown-up beside them, or write one sentence), then share. Answer: neither is right. A 2 by 3 rectangle has 6 squares and does not fold (it has a 2 by 2 block), so Mei is wrong. A row of 4 with one square above each end does not fold either, because both extra squares cover the back, so Ben is wrong. One counter-example is enough to show a rule is not always true. Not asked in class (moved to the Weekly Dose of Thinking Math): "How can you tell two faces are opposite without folding?" (They are the ends of a line of 3 squares.) Praise the reasons, not only the answers. TALK TIME RULES: ask for volunteers first; a child may always pass; never call on a child by name to answer; no public tallies, scores or rankings; praise the reason, not the child. If a question uses a mistaken idea (a character who gets it wrong), present it as the character's idea and ask "What would you say to them?" so no child owns the mistake.
