Explore: talk about it

1. How much pizza does each of the 4 friends get? Write it as a division and as a fraction.

2. How did you cut so that each friend got only 2 pieces?

3. Could you do 5 pizzas among 6 friends with 2 pieces each?

GO FURTHER: A SECOND WHAT-IF (OPTIONAL, FOR ANY PAIR)

4 pizzas are shared among 5 children, 5 pizzas among 6 children, and 6 pizzas among 7 children. Who gets the biggest share? What do you notice as the group gets bigger?

Math Inquirers P5 Holiday Program • Notice it. Test it. Explain why.

Minutes 14 to 21. Gather the pairs. Answers: 1) 3 ÷ 4 = 3/4 of a pizza each (cut every pizza into 4: 12 quarters, 3 each). 2) Cut 2 pizzas into halves and 1 pizza into quarters: each friend gets 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4, which is 2 pieces. 3) Yes: cut 3 pizzas into halves (6 halves) and 2 pizzas into thirds (6 thirds): each friend gets 1/2 + 1/3 = 5/6. Say: "This is the same as writing 5/6 as a sum of two different unit fractions: 1/2 + 1/3." That is the puzzle type on the worksheet, but with different numbers. Do not give away the worksheet fractions. GO FURTHER what-if (open to every pair from minute 9; nobody waits): 4/5, 5/6 and 6/7: the share grows each time (0.8, 0.83, 0.86). Each child is 1/5, 1/6, 1/7 short of a whole pizza, a smaller piece each time, so the shares creep up towards 1 pizza but never reach it.
