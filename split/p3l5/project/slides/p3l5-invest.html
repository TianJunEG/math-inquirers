Investigation: Is it always true? Three in a row

Write any three whole numbers in a row, like 14, 15, 16. Exactly one of them is in the 3 times table (15).

1. Try 5 different starting numbers (make one of them bigger than 100). Circle the number in the 3 times table each time. Is it always exactly one?

2. Explain why. Think about the remainders when you divide by 3.

3. What if we change it? Try 4 numbers in a row and the 4 times table. Then try only 2 numbers in a row and the 3 times table.

Easy first step: circle the number in the 3 times table in 14, 15, 16. Then try 20, 21, 22.

Minutes 67 to 72: the Investigation has its OWN slot. Show this slide to the whole group and invite everybody: "You can choose Think harder, Go further or the Investigation now. Nobody has to." Do not say who is on which one. Not finished at 72? Take it home and bring one thing to share at the start of next lesson (volunteers only). DEBRIEF in talk time, minutes 72 to 75: ask for volunteers to share one thing they noticed (a pass is fine). Then show the model answer and ask "What stayed the same? What changed? Why?" Model answer and what to look for: Always true: among 3 numbers in a row there is exactly one in the 3 times table. Remainders when dividing by 3 go 1, 2, 0, 1, 2, 0, ... so in any 3 in a row each remainder 0, 1, 2 appears once, and remainder 0 means it is in the table. With 4 in a row and the 4 times table it is also exactly one (remainders 0, 1, 2, 3 repeat). With only 2 in a row and the 3 times table it is NOT always true (4 and 5 have none). In general, n numbers in a row always have exactly one in the n times table. Look for: several tests including a big number, the remainder cycle as the reason, and a counterexample for the 2 in a row case.
