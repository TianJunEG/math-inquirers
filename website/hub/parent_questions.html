Questions parents ask us about Primary 1 maths

Straight answers from our teachers on getting ready for P1, the topics your child will meet, helping at home and stretching strong learners.

Ask a question

We answer the most common questions here. Your name is never shown.

Short answer: Primary 1 maths covers numbers to 100, adding and subtracting, a first look at multiplication and division, shapes, length, time, money and picture graphs.

A typical order across the year (schools sequence topics a little differently):

  • Semester 1: numbers to 10, adding and subtracting within 10, shapes, ordinal numbers (1st, 2nd, 3rd), numbers to 20, adding and subtracting within 20, picture graphs, numbers to 100.
  • Semester 2: adding and subtracting within 100, length, multiplication, division, time and money.

Since the 2021 syllabus, P1 children also measure length in centimetres, tell time to 5 minutes and use a.m. and p.m., so these come up earlier than many parents remember.

Source: Radin Mas Primary, P1 Maths parent briefing 2024

Short answer: Most children are ready if they can count objects to 20 reliably, read and write numbers to 20, and stay with a short task for 10 to 15 minutes.

Signs to look for:

  • Counts out 17 objects without skipping or double counting.
  • Says one more and one less than a number without starting from 1.
  • Splits 7 objects into two groups in more than one way.
  • Continues a simple pattern and can say the rule.

If a few of these are shaky, that is normal for K2. A few weeks of short, playful practice usually closes the gap. We offer a free 15-minute readiness check for K2 children before they join Math Inquirers.

Short answer: Keep it short, playful and hands-on: counting real objects, number bonds to 10, and talking about maths in daily life.

  • Count things you use every day: spoons, steps, buttons. Then group them in 2s and 5s.
  • Play "make 10": how many ways can 10 blocks be split into two groups?
  • Let your child pay for something small with coins and count the change together.
  • Practise writing numbers 0 to 20 neatly, especially ones that are often reversed, like 3, 7, 12 and 21.

Ten minutes a day is plenty. Stop while it is still fun.

Short answer: Counting aloud is a good start, but Primary 1 needs number sense: knowing what numbers mean and how they relate.

A child who can recite to 100 may still struggle to say what comes before 40, or which is bigger, 59 or 61. Check that your child can:

  • Count on from any number, such as from 47 to 53.
  • Count back, such as from 30 to 22.
  • Explain why 61 is more than 59 (6 tens is more than 5 tens).
  • Show 34 as 3 tens and 4 ones with objects or a drawing.

Short answer: A number bond shows how a number splits into two parts, for example 7 is 5 and 2, or 4 and 3. It is the foundation for adding and subtracting in Singapore maths.

Children usually draw a number bond as a circle for the whole joined to two circles for the parts. Learning all the bonds for 10 (1 and 9, 2 and 8, and so on) makes mental maths much faster later: 8 + 5 becomes 8 + 2 + 3 = 13.

At home, use 10 small objects and two cups. Hide some under one cup and ask how many are hidden.

Source: Radin Mas Primary, P1 Maths parent briefing 2024

Short answer: Teach "=" as "is the same as", not "the answer comes next". Both sides must have the same value.

Many children think 4 + 4 = 8 + 1 is fine because "4 + 4 is 8". Ask: "Is 4 + 4 the same as 8 + 1?" Use a balance or two piles of objects to check. Questions like 5 + 3 = __ + 4 build this idea well.

Short answer: Start with objects in groups, then move to a number line, then to saying the pattern.

  1. Put buttons in groups of 2 and count the groups: 2, 4, 6, 8.
  2. Hop along a number line in 2s, 5s or 10s and say each landing number.
  3. Ask "What will come next? How do you know?" so your child spots the rule.

Skip counting leads straight into multiplication in Semester 2.

Short answer: Children learn the Singapore coins and notes, count amounts, and make the same amount in different ways.

A good home activity is "how many ways can you make 20 cents?" with real coins. Encourage your child to start with the biggest coin and work down, so no way is missed or repeated. Our Coin Combos lesson builds on exactly this idea.

Short answer: A picture graph shows data with small pictures, where each picture stands for one item (or later, more than one).

P1 children read picture graphs and answer questions like "how many more chose apple than banana?". Make one together: ask family members their favourite fruit and draw one fruit per vote.

Example: "I think of a number. I add 4 and get 11. What is my number?" Undo the "add 4" by taking away 4: 11 − 4 = 7. Check: 7 + 4 = 11. This strategy is common in challenging word problems later on, and P1 children can start with simple one-step versions.

Short answer: For a Primary 1 child, 10 to 15 focused minutes most days works better than one long session a week.

Short sessions keep confidence high. Mix one quick skill (like number bonds) with one puzzle that needs thinking. If your child is tired or upset, stop and try again tomorrow.

Short answer: Ask "How do you know?" every time, including when the answer is right. Children quickly learn that the reason matters.

Give objects or a drawing to check with, and praise the checking: "I like how you counted to make sure." If guessing continues, the question may be too hard, so step back to a smaller number.

Short answer: Use the school method as the main way, and show your own way as a second method to compare.

Different methods can confuse a young child if introduced at the same time. Ask your child to show you how they learned it in school first. Comparing two methods later is a great thinking exercise.

Short answer: Talk maths during ordinary moments: shopping, cooking, travelling and playing.

  • At the shop: "Which is cheaper? By how much?"
  • In the kitchen: "We need 3 eggs for each cake. How many for 2 cakes?"
  • On the MRT: "How many stops to go? How many have we passed?"
  • With toys: sort them, count them, and make patterns.

Short answer: Make it safe to be stuck: struggling a little is how thinking grows.

Give a smaller step rather than the answer: "Can you try it with objects?" or "What would happen with a smaller number?" Share that you also get stuck sometimes. End on a question your child can do so they finish feeling capable.

Short answer: Go deeper rather than faster: give puzzles that need reasoning, not just next year's topics.

Good stretch tasks ask a child to find every possible answer, explain why something works, or work backwards. For example: "Find all the ways to make 30 cents with 5, 10 and 20 cent coins." This uses ideas from approaches common in gifted education (depth, reasoning and explaining why) and extends thinking beyond the school syllabus without racing ahead of it.

Short answer: Some are, if they are chosen for thinking rather than speed and your child enjoys them.

Good olympiad-style tasks for P1 involve patterns, logic clues, listing all possibilities and simple working backwards. Avoid timed competitions at this age; the goal is curiosity and persistence. Watch for signs your child is ready: enjoys puzzles, explains reasons, and keeps trying when stuck.
